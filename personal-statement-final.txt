I noticed he was gone the way you notice a clock has stopped. I hadn't been watching him. I had been counting on him.

He was a boy I raced in the 50-meter freestyle, one of the familiar faces in Taipei's small swimming community. After every heat, he talked eagerly about his splits, the time for each length of the race, to anyone who would listen. I listened for one number only: his finish. That number was my target. I never once asked him how a race had felt. So when he stopped showing up, I didn't miss him. I missed having something to chase.

That bothered me more than I expected, because I knew exactly where the habit came from.

It started on a pool deck. I couldn't finish a butterfly set that my teammates made look effortless, and I ended up sprawled on the tiles, listening to the muffled laughter around me. In that moment I learned what it felt like to be reduced to one value. How fast I could swim was the only measurement that mattered.

I almost quit. That night I lay awake, frustrated that one bad set got to decide what I was worth. I wanted my worth to come from choices I made. So I kept swimming. My times dropped, and every fraction of a second became evidence I could hold up against that laughter. Somewhere along the way, I started needing someone ahead of me to prove it against. He became that someone.

When I checked the roster, I saw he wasn't the only one gone. Familiar names were disappearing, and I finally looked at the system behind them. The International High School Athletic Association (IHSAA) offered one swim meet a year instead of a season, so a single race was the only official record of a year of work. In a system like that, every swimmer becomes a number, and the swimmer next to you becomes a target. I had done to him exactly what that laughter had done to me. I could also see where it ended. There would always be another boy at another meet with a faster time, and I would never be done chasing.

I couldn't change the IHSAA. I could build something next to it.

I founded the Taiwan Inter-school Athletics League (TISAL) to give swimmers more than one chance a year to race. I negotiated with the National Defense Medical Center to rent its pool during off-peak hours. I built an automated timing pipeline, and I designed a five-tier performance framework so that a time became a checkpoint for growth instead of a verdict on worth, a record that could keep changing at every meet. A swimmer who climbed a tier had proof of progress that one bad race could not erase.

Then I tried the part I was least sure about. Public and international schools in Taipei share the same city but almost never the same starting block. For our first meet, I invited a local public school into a pool full of international school swimmers who had never raced them.

It was awkward at first. Then it was not.

Over the season, swimmers who had arrived in separate groups started sitting together. Under the old system, you got one time, accepted it as the final word, and walked away in silence. At TISAL, swimmers crowded around the results comparing splits and trading strategies, the way that boy used to after every heat. The difference was that now someone was listening.

My pipeline recorded every race we swam. It never recorded the thing that actually changed: a deck full of swimmers from different schools who had stopped treating each other as numbers to beat. I still watch the clock when I race. I just don't need it to tell me who I am anymore.
